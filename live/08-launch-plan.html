It answers five questions in order of how fast a wrong answer kills the company: harassment, empty rooms, the job wedge, onboarding, money. It ends with GTM, the cold-start arithmetic, and the first 90 days.

Every number is tagged (V) verified, (D) derived from a stated assumption, or (H) hypothesis to be measured. The previous plan's mistake was presenting model output as strategy; this one presents assumptions as assumptions.

0. The brutal version

Five things are true and the plan only works if we say them out loud.

  1. AI does not solve cold start. It solves the wait. If AI is more than 15% of minutes, we have built a chatbot with a queue animation, and the human network never forms. AI is a bridge, capped and labelled.
  2. Group rooms are a liquidity liability, not a feature. One table of four consumes the supply of four 1:1s. It ships after 1:1 works, not with it — regardless of what the prototype demonstrates.
  3. Verification proves accountability, not character. A verified predator is still a predator. The product's safety comes from removing private surfaces (no DMs, no profiles), ending calls in one tap, and reviewing fast. Verification only raises the cost of coming back.
  4. Removing DMs will cost retention. HelloTalk's DM loop is why people open it daily. We replace it with live sessions, a recap, and a reason to return within 48 hours — not with text. If retention collapses without DMs, the answer is scheduled live sessions, never a chat feature.
  5. A 10-minute call is not a resume asset. It is a rep. The job-seeker product sells reps and a record of them; claiming more is the fastest way to lose the segment.

1. Harassment and ghosting — the Anti-Toxicity PRD

1.1 Remove the surface, then police the remainder

HelloTalk's harassment problem is not a moderation problem. It is a surface problem: profiles to browse, photos to judge, location to infer, and private messages to send without a witness. You cannot moderate a DM you never see.

HelloTalk surface Why it produces harassment What Eloq does instead
Direct messages Private, asynchronous, no witness, no cost to send No text channel exists between users. Not disabled — absent
Profiles (photo, bio, city, age) Selection and targeting; appearance becomes the product Display name only, chosen at signup. No photo, bio, city, employer, or links
Followers / friends Turns a conversation into an audience and a return path No follows, no friend list, no contact exchange prompt
Location Proximity as a pretext; doxxing risk Never collected, never inferred in UI
Open-ended calls Endless rooms, no exit ritual, no shared expectation 10-minute timebox with a designed ending
Reporting buried in settings The victim has to work to be safe One tap from the room, ends the call before it asks anything

The design position: make the creepy behaviour structurally useless. There is no place to send an unsolicited photo, no profile to judge, and no way to contact someone after the call. What remains — live voice — is witnessed by both parties, timeboxed, and reportable in one tap.

1.2 The report flow, exactly

The rule from the prototype is the rule: the call ends first, the question comes second. A person who is being harassed should not have to choose a category while the offender is still in their ears.

[ … ] → Report

   ├─ 0.0s  Call drops for both. No confirm dialog. No "are you sure".
   │        The other side sees "Call ended" — never "You were reported".
   │
   ├─ 0.2s  Black screen. Soft green check.
   │        "Call ended."  /  "What went wrong?"

   ├─ 0.3s  Three rows, full width, 56px:

   │           They left early
   │           Technical problem
   │
   ├─ tap Inappropriate → one optional line ("Anything else we should know?")
   │        + "Attach the last 30 seconds" (only if the safety buffer was on)
   │        + Submit  →  Trust & Safety P1 queue
   │
   └─ tap the other two → no moderation case. Product feedback only.
Rule Detail Why
No pre-confirmation Tapping Report ends the call instantly A confirmation is a chance to talk the victim out of it
No category quiz Three options, not six The victim is not a classifier; T&S triages from the clip and history
Report and block are different Block is silent, instant, permanent, pair-level. Report creates a case Most people want the person gone, not an investigation

Exact copy, the whole screen:

Call ended. What went wrong?

Inappropriate › They left early › Technical problem ›

Nothing — just leave

The green check is not celebration; it is the signal that the call is over and the user is safe. No red, no alarm sound, no "we take this seriously" paragraph.

What happens next, in the background:

  1. The pair is excluded from matching before the report is even submitted (at the moment the call drops).
  2. If the option is Inappropriate, the reported account enters protective exclusion (definition below) within 60 seconds.
  3. A human reviewer sees the case with: account age, prior reports, upheld history, the optional clip, and the reporter's account (never exposed to the reported user).
  4. The reporter never matches the reported user again, regardless of outcome.

1.3 "Shadow-ban" done honestly — and where I refuse the brief

A global silent shadow-ban — the offender keeps using the app with everyone except the reporter — is the industry's favourite safety theatre. It protects the one person who complained and quietly feeds the predator to the next one. We do not do that.

Three distinct mechanisms, three different names:

Mechanism Scope Duration Visible to offender? Why
Pair exclusion Reporter ↔ reported Permanent No The victim never sees them again. Non-negotiable.
Protective exclusion Same language pair + age band + launch metro 24h, or until review No Limits the blast radius while a human decides. Bounded, reviewed.
Suspension / ban Device + phone hash, all matching 7 days / permanent Yes, at the next login The actual consequence. Visible, appealable, recorded.

The brief asked for a shadow-ban "from matching with that demographic". That is the middle row: protective exclusion, capped at 24 hours, never indefinite, and always followed by a human decision. An indefinite silent ban is how a company avoids confronting its own report queue.

Enforcement ladder:

Tier Trigger Immediate action Review SLA Outcome if upheld
P1 Sexual content, threats, hate speech, suspected minor, credible scam Removed from matching within 60s; session evidence preserved ≤ 4 hours, 24/5 Permanent ban, device-level; law enforcement where required
P2 Repeated off-topic solicitation, recording without consent, evasion Removed from matching; case opened ≤ 24 hours 7-day suspension → permanent on repeat
P3 Left early, technical, "something else" No action on the other user Feedback only Product fix, not a punishment
  • Appeals: one human appeal for P1/P2. Outcome and reason recorded. Appeal uphold rate target < 15%.
  • Staffing: 2 FTE Trust & Safety at launch, 24/5 coverage, on-call for minor safety. One reviewer per ~25k MAU (H), measured by queue depth, not ratio.
  • Public transparency: a quarterly report with report volume, P1 action times, bans, and appeals overturned. This is a trust moat HelloTalk cannot copy without admitting its numbers.

1.4 Verification without the friction tax

The mistake is verifying at install. At install, the user has received nothing, so every second of verification is a reason to leave. Verify at the moment value begins.

Stage Check Cost What it unlocks Why here
Install Nothing. No account, no email, no password $0 Welcome screen only The first tap must test the promise
Before room 1 Phone OTP (hashed, never displayed) + device attestation ~$0.02–0.05 1:1 and tables A reachable adult behind every voice; raises the cost of a throwaway account
Before room 3 Selfie liveness + age estimation (Persona/Onfido) ~$0.60–1.20 Continued matching, tables The user has sunk value; only retained users cost money; catches adults posing as teens and teens posing as adults
Trusted 10 completed rooms, 0 upheld reports, 30 days $0 May open a table; new-user pairing Supply-side responsibility earned by behaviour, never shown as a badge

Two rules make this safe enough to defer the selfie:

  1. Rooms 1–2 pair the new user only with Trusted users. The most vulnerable moment (a brand-new account meeting a stranger) is matched with the most accountable cohort. The partner sees a quiet "New here" line; no one sees a status trophy.
  2. Any signal reopens verification immediately. A report mentioning a minor, a voice that contradicts the stated age, or a device linked to a banned account removes the user from matching pending review — regardless of their self-declared age.

What we collect: birth year (not date), hashed phone, display name, language pair, device attestation token. What we never collect: real name, email (unless the user later adds it for cross-device), photo, city, employer, contacts, social handles, date of birth, precise location.

The honest limit: liveness proves there is one consistent adult human, not that the human is safe. It stops ban evasion and casual impersonation. It does not stop a first-time offender, which is why the report flow and the no-DM surface carry more weight than the check itself.

1.5 The ghosting fix: the AI is a bridge, not a destination

Ghosting in a live product has three shapes. Each gets a different fix.

Shape What the user experiences Fix
No one there (cold queue) "Finding a Spanish speaker…" for 10s Inline AI fallback, never a modal
Partner vanishes Mid-sentence silence, then "Mateo left" AI takes over in < 2s, same topic, same timer
Partner is silent 60s of dead air Directed prompt to the silent side; after 90s, offer a graceful exit

The 10-second rule. At 10s the queue tells the truth and changes state in place:

No one free right now. Ana can practise with you until a person arrives.

[ Keep waiting for a person ] ← primary Practise with AI while you wait ← secondary

The AI is the second choice. A person is still the product. The screen never dead-ends and never lies about who is speaking.

The handoff contract. When a human becomes available while an AI room is live:

  1. The AI says one line: "Mateo is free. I'll hand you over — your topic carries across."
  2. A 3-second crossfade, with a single "Stay with Ana" escape. No new matching screen, no re-handshake, no timer reset.
  3. The human receives a one-line brief, not a transcript: topic, level band, and one phrase the user was practising. No recording, no history, no profile.
  4. If the user declines ("Stay with Ana"), the handoff does not happen and the AI continues to the session cap.

The AI's behaviour, so it does not become a lesson:

  • Same topic prompt as a human room; one question at a time; waits 1.5s before filling silence.
  • Speaks at the user's band, not native speed.
  • At most one correction every 90 seconds, through the same cue card as a human correction.
  • Never claims to be human. Labelled "Ana · AI partner" in the room and by a badge on the circle.
  • Hard cap: 5 minutes per session, 5 minutes per day on free. At the cap: "I'll keep your place. Want to try a person again?"

What we measure, because AI can hide a dead network:

Metric Target Action if missed
AI share of total minutes ≤ 15% Above 20% for 2 weeks: cut AI hours or concentrate supply; do not celebrate AI minutes
Human P90 match time ≤ 15s at peak Above 25s: shrink the matching window or drop a pair
Handoff acceptance ≥ 70% Below 50%: the AI is too good or the human intro is clumsy — fix the intro copy first
Post-handoff completion ≥ 80% Below 70%: the handoff feels like a bait-and-switch

The brutal read: AI fallback is a retention bandage over a supply wound. It buys 8–12 weeks to build liquidity. If the human match rate is not improving while AI minutes grow, we are building the wrong product and the dashboard will say so.

2. Empty rooms — the Clubhouse fix

2.1 The timebox: 10 minutes, every format

Format Length Structure Why
1:1 10:00 hard One topic, two voices, one correction Long enough to warm up and say something real; short enough that a bad match costs 10 minutes, not an evening
Table 10:00 hard 0:30 intro + 3 × 3:00 rounds + 0:30 outro Everyone speaks in every round; the end is predictable
Open room Does not exist — This is the Clubhouse failure: a room with no end has no reason to start

No extensions at launch. An extension button converts a designed ending into an infinite room and destroys the supply math. If two people want more, the recap offers "Talk again" — a new session, a new match, a new 10 minutes.

Why 10 and not 5: five minutes is a greeting plus one question. There is no time for a correction to land or for either person to relax. Ten is the smallest unit that produces the feeling of a conversation; fifteen recreates Clubhouse.

2.2 The countdown that does not feel like a bomb

The timer is information, not pressure. Rules:

Moment What the UI does What it never does
10:00 → 2:00 A 1px progress hairline under the pair label; the numeric timer is muted, tabular, 13px No red, no pulsing, no per-second animation
2:00 left One quiet line, same muted style: "Two minutes left" No chime, no colour change
0:20 left Prompt card swaps to: "One thing you'll do differently next time." No countdown beep, no "hurry"
0:00 Soft two-note chime; screen fades to recap; both sides see "Session complete" No abrupt cut, no "connection lost"
Recap 3 facts + Done; "Talk again" is the one secondary action No confetti, no streak, no share

The ending is a feature, not an interruption. A designed ending is what makes a 10-minute session repeatable; an open-ended room is what makes people leave without a goodbye.

2.3 Speaking balance: structure, not surveillance

The group room is a cluster of equal circles. No stage, no audience count, no speaker panel, no host. Everyone in the room is a participant; there is no listener role to hide in.

The round is the enforcement. Turn-taking is enforced by the format, not by muting adults:

  • Round 1 (3:00): the least-spoken person leads. The app highlights the next speaker with a ring and one line: "Lucía, your turn."
  • Round 2 (3:00): rotation to the next least-spoken. The lead picks the next question from the prompt card.
  • Round 3 (3:00): open conversation, with the balance meter visible.
  • Pass is one tap and carries no penalty. Silence is allowed; being put on the spot is not.

The signals, and their limits:

Signal What it shows What it does not show
Three dots under a person The last three turns that person took Rank, score, popularity
Speaking ring Who is talking now Who is "best"
Balance meter Share of airtime, one bar A grade
Dominance nudge "Give someone else a turn?" — shown only to the dominant speaker, for 10s, after 3 of the last 4 turns or > 45% airtime A public callout, a mute, a punishment
Silence prompt After 12s of no speech, a question directed at the quietest person "You're being quiet"

Why we do not auto-mute the dominant speaker: it is paternalistic, it breaks accessibility (some people need longer turns), and it turns a conversation into a conference line. The nudge plus the round structure changes behaviour without a controller. If the nudge fails, the round ends; that is the enforcement.

Group launch gate (non-negotiable):

Condition Threshold
1:1 P90 match time ≤ 15s at peak, 4 consecutive weeks
Peak concurrent users in the pair ≥ 300
Table fill rate ≥ 80% within 20s of opening
1:1 D7 ≥ 30%

Until all four hold, tables stay in the prototype and the waitlist. Shipping group early is how a liquidity problem becomes a ghost-town story.

3. The job-seeker wedge

3.1 Who we are actually serving

Two audiences, one product:

  • Students (18–24): free, casual, price-sensitive, high frequency, low willingness to pay. They are the supply that keeps evenings alive.
  • Job seekers (22–35): dated goal ("interview in 6 weeks"), high willingness to pay, low frequency, high stakes. They are the revenue and the reason the product has an outcome.

The home screen does not split them into modes. The topic chips do the segmentation: Interview, Everyday, Exam. One product, three intents.

The wedge is peer mock interviews, not coaching. Coaching is one-way, expensive, and turns us into a course. Two job seekers interviewing each other is reciprocal, free to supply, and closer to the real thing.

3.2 The three rooms

Room Format Prompts (examples) Recap artifact Tier
Mock Interview 1:1, 10 min, swap roles at 5:00 "Tell me about yourself." · "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager." · "Why this role?" 3 questions asked, your strongest 60s answer, 1 fix, 1 model phrase Free: generic pack. Career: role packs + record
Workplace Small Talk 1:1 or table of 4, 10 min Standup update · coffee chat · "Can I ask you something?" · saying no · asking for help 1 phrase you avoided, 1 phrase to use next time Free
Salary Negotiation 1:1, 10 min, 4 scripted beats Offer → counter → silence → close. "That's a little lower than I expected." The number you said, the number you avoided, one stronger sentence Career

Why Small Talk is free and in the wedge: it is the highest-frequency anxiety for non-native speakers at work, and it needs no role pack. It also keeps the job-seeker product from becoming a once-a-year purchase.

3.3 Value capture: a practice record, not a resume badge

The recap stays exactly as locked: three facts, one Done button. The paid artifact is generated quietly in the background and lives outside the moment.

The Practice Record (Career):

  • Date, role, and room type.
  • The 3 questions asked.
  • Your best 60-second answer, transcribed (opt-in at first save).
  • One fix and one model phrase.
  • One prompt for the next session.
  • Export as PDF or a private link. Delete in one tap. Retained 90 days by default.

What it is not:

  • Not a score, percentile, "readiness %", or badge.
  • Not a claim that the user is ready for an interview.
  • Not shareable to LinkedIn at launch (a public brag invites gaming and misrepresents a 10-minute rep).
  • Not a replacement for the recap. The call screen never becomes a dashboard.

Why this is the honest version of "resume asset": interviews are won by having said the answer out loud, repeatedly. The record is evidence of reps and a script to rehearse — useful, tangible, and true. Calling it a credential would be a lie that the first hiring manager exposes.

Kill signal: if fewer than 8% of Interview-topic users start a second mock within 14 days, the wedge is a topic chip, not a business. Keep it free and stop building Career.

4.3 The verification calendar

Why the phone first: it is the cheapest credible proof that a real adult is behind the account, it survives reinstalls via hash, and it does not require a selfie before the user has heard a single voice. Why the selfie later: it is the highest-friction step, and by room 3 the user has a reason to stay and the platform has a reason to pay for the check.

4.4 Activation is two rooms, not one

The first room proves the promise; the second proves the habit. The product pushes one thing after the recap: "Talk again" — same topic, new partner, one tap. The first-session target is two rooms in seven days, not one.

Notifications are asked for after the first recap, with the only message we will ever send by default: "Your practice window is open — 6 people are free now." One per week, opt-in, off by default. No streak loss, no "someone is waiting" if it is not true.

5. Monetization that does not break the room

5.1 Principles

  1. Sell time, priority, and proof. Never safety, never corrections, never the ability to leave, never the recap's three facts.
  2. No ads, ever. An ad in a 10-minute language call is the end of the brand.
  3. Never enforce the cap mid-conversation. A call that reaches the limit finishes; the next join is blocked; the paywall appears after the recap.
  4. Priority never bypasses verification. It reorders people who passed the same gate.

Two corrections to the earlier plan:

  • The recap is not paywalled. 05 proposed "Full (words, 2 fixes, next step)" for Plus. That contradicts the locked three-fact recap and turns the learning loop into a demo. Everyone gets the same recap; Career sells the record and the role packs.
  • The AI is not a paid feature first. It is the fallback that protects the free experience. Plus gets more of it, but no one is ever charged to avoid an empty queue.

5.3 Why these prices

  • $12.99 / $79.99 is exact parity with HelloTalk VIP (V, 2026-11-15). Parity makes the comparison one sentence: same price, and it actually connects you. Undercutting at $9.99 signals a weaker product.
  • $24.99 Career anchors against a human interview coach ($100–300/session), not against a language app. It is the only tier where role-specific content and a record justify a different price.
  • Annual is the default toggle, monthly one tap away. Annual funds verification upfront and is the retention product.

5.4 Unit economics and the AI trap

Line Estimate Basis

The AI trap, stated plainly: 5 free AI minutes/day × 30 days × $0.06 = $9/month per user who maxes it — more than a Plus subscription. The cap only works if usage is rare. Guard: AI share ≤ 15% of minutes; if free AI COGS exceeds $0.60/MAU/month, cut the free cap to 3 min/day before touching price.

Illustrative margin at 200 human minutes/month:

Break-even conversion ≈ 3.6% of monthly actives. That is the business: a cheap live product with a generous free tier, not a $5/minute AI tutor.

5.5 The North Star and the guardrails

North Star: Weekly Human Speaking Minutes per Weekly Active User (WHSM/WAU).

Measured by voice-activity detection on the user's own speech, in rooms with a verified human partner. AI minutes, listening time, and room duration are excluded.

Why this metric Why not the alternatives
It measures the actual behaviour the product exists to produce: the user speaking, with a human, repeatedly Total minutes grows with marketing, long calls, and AI; it says nothing about habit
It is per active user, so it cannot be inflated by buying traffic DAU rewards streaks and daily guilt in a product designed for 3×/week
It is human-only, so AI cannot fake it Sessions rewards 30-second joins; words rewards button-mashing

Targets: 30 min by week 12 · 40 min by month 6 · activation (2 human rooms in 7 days) ≥ 45%.

Guardrails, with actions:

Guardrail Threshold Action if breached
AI share of minutes ≤ 15% > 20% for 2 weeks: cut AI hours, concentrate supply, do not grow
Report rate ≤ 3.0 / 1k room-minutes, falling Freeze growth; audit matching

Counter-metrics against mindless clicking: average session length 8–12 minutes (not 25), sessions per user per week ≤ 6 (monitor, don't cap), and the ratio of completed human rooms to AI rooms. A dashboard that celebrates total minutes while the human ratio falls is celebrating the wrong product.

6.6 The cold-start arithmetic (the part most plans hide)

A 10-minute session, a two-sided queue, and a 10-second wait imply a lot of simultaneous supply. Using Little's Law as a rough planning model:

  • With N users online in a pair at peak and 10-minute sessions, each side produces N/20 arrivals per minute.
  • A 10-second median wait needs roughly 120+ concurrent users per pair, and a 15-second P90 needs a buffer of 200–300 — because some of those users are already in calls, not waiting. (D)
  • 300 concurrent users at 10 minutes each is 1,800 sessions per hour — which is why four concentrated hours beat 24/7 coverage with the same DAU.

What this means for the plan:

  1. 300 concurrent users at 8pm is the minimum viable liquidity, drawn from a roster of roughly 600 founding speakers (half show up on a given night). At 150 per side, waits are measured in tens of seconds, not units.
  2. A 500–1,000 MAU beta can sustain one pair for four hours a day. It cannot sustain two pairs or 24 hours. Every extra pair divides the same supply.
  3. AI covers the gaps, but it does not create the network. If 30% of peak sessions are AI, the human queue is too thin and the "match in seconds" promise is being kept by a machine.
  4. The 10-second claim is a supply promise, not an engineering one. If we cannot staff 18:00–23:00, we say "peak hours" on Home rather than fake a countdown.

Three honest levers, in order: concentrate hours → narrow pairs → seed recruit. Paid marketing before liquidity buys people a bad first impression at scale.
